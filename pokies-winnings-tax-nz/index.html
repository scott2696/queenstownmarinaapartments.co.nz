Gambling Winnings Tax NZ · Do You Pay GST on Pokies Wins?

It's the question every Kiwi asks after a decent win — do I owe the taxman a cut? The short answer for recreational players is no. Below we explain why, the one edge case where tax can apply, how offshore and crypto winnings are treated, and the records worth keeping just in case.

Quick answer: No — recreational gambling winnings are not taxed in New Zealand. There is no income tax and no GST on a normal pokies or casino win, whether the site is local or offshore. You don't declare it and nothing is deducted when you cash out. The only real exceptions: a genuine professional gambler running a gambling business, and the crypto nuance — the win is tax-free, but IRD treats the coins themselves as taxable property.

Why gambling winnings aren't taxed in New Zealand

New Zealand's tax system taxes income. A recreational gambling win isn't treated as income — it's the product of chance, not of work or a business. Because there's no reliable way to "earn" from gambling over time (the house holds the edge), IRD doesn't treat an ordinary player's winnings as taxable, and there's no capital gains tax that would catch them either.

So when you win on a pokie, hit a table game, or land a sports bet as a punter, the money is simply yours. You don't file it, you don't declare it, and no tax is withheld at the point you withdraw. The same logic means you also can't claim your gambling losses as a deduction — it cuts both ways.

How each type of gambling is treated

The tax-free rule is broad, but it helps to see it applied to each activity Kiwis actually play. For a recreational punter, all of the below are treated the same way — as windfalls from chance, not income.

Pokies and online casino wins

Whether you're playing a pub pokie, a NZ casino floor or an online slot at an offshore site, a recreational win is not taxed. No income tax, no GST on your win, nothing to declare. The same applies to table games like blackjack and roulette.

TAB betting on racing and sport

Winnings from a bet placed with the TAB — the only licensed New Zealand betting operator — are treated the same as any recreational gambling win: tax-free for the punter. The TAB itself pays levies and duties as an operator, but those obligations don't pass through to your payout. See our sports betting NZ guide for how the market works.

Lotto, Powerball and lottery prizes

Lotto and Powerball prizes are the clearest windfalls of all — pure chance — and they're not taxable income in New Zealand. Whether you win a few dollars or a multi-million-dollar Powerball jackpot, you receive the full prize with no tax withheld. What changes once the money lands is covered in the interest section below.

Poker

Recreational poker winnings sit in the same tax-free category. Poker raises the professional-gambler question more often than pokies do, because skill plays a larger role — but for someone playing casually, or even seriously as a hobby, winnings remain untaxed. The line is about whether the activity has become a business, not about how good you are.

Interest and investment income on your winnings

This is where a genuinely tax-free win can still create a tax bill later — and it catches lottery winners out. The prize is tax-free. But the moment you put it to work — a bank account, a term deposit, shares, a rental property — the interest and investment returns it generates are ordinary taxable income, taxed exactly like any other interest or investment earnings.

So a $1 million Powerball win arrives untaxed, but the interest that million earns each year is taxable in the normal way. This is a common point of confusion in "how much tax do you pay on $100,000 in NZ" style questions: the lump sum isn't taxed, the income it later produces is. If you win big, that's the part worth planning for.

The biggest lottery-winner mistake: most advisers agree it's rushing — spending fast, telling everyone, and skipping advice — rather than anything tax-specific. The win is tax-free; treating it like a windfall to protect, not an income to spend, is the practical lesson.

When prize money is taxable

There's a specific corner of the rules worth knowing, because it's where "prizes are tax-free" stops applying. When prize money is won as part of a taxable activity — a business, a profession or a trade — it can become taxable. IRD's own guidance gives the examples:

  • A professional in their field — a professional athlete's competition winnings, or an author winning a literary prize, can be taxable because they're connected to how that person earns a living.
  • A business context — prize money a farmer wins at an agricultural show tied to their farming business can be taxable.
  • Sporting event withholding — organisers must deduct withholding tax from larger prize payments to participants competing professionally; there's a threshold per participant per event.
  • Endorsements and appearance fees — money earned around a win (sponsorship, appearances) is income, separate from the win itself.

None of this touches a recreational pokies, Lotto or sports-betting win. It matters only where the "prize" is really the product of someone's work or business — which is the same principle behind the professional-gambler edge case below.

Is there GST on gambling in New Zealand?

Not for you. This is where a lot of confusion creeps in. GST does exist in the gambling system — New Zealand gambling operators account for GST on their gaming revenue as part of how the sector is taxed. But that's the operator's obligation, not yours. As a player, when you withdraw a win, no GST is taken out of it. The number you win is the number you keep.

The professional gambler edge case

There is one situation where gambling can become taxable: when it's run as a genuine business rather than recreation. If someone gambles in an organised, systematic way, with a clear profit-making system, and effectively relies on it as their trade, Inland Revenue may treat the profits as taxable income.

The bar for this is high, and it's deliberately rare. Playing often, or even winning a lot, doesn't make you a professional gambler for tax purposes. It's about whether the activity has the character of a business. For the overwhelming majority of Kiwis — anyone playing for entertainment — winnings stay firmly untaxed.

If you think this might be you: the line between a serious hobby and a gambling business is genuinely grey, and the consequences of getting it wrong matter. Get advice from a chartered accountant or check directly with Inland Revenue rather than guessing.

Offshore winnings — treated the same

Because Kiwis currently play at offshore online casinos, a common worry is whether a win from an overseas site is somehow taxable back home. It isn't. The tax treatment follows the nature of the win — a recreational gambling win — not the location of the operator. A recreational win from an offshore casino is treated exactly like a win from any New Zealand gambling: not taxed. The crypto nuance — the win is free, the coin is property

Crypto is the one area where "gambling winnings aren't taxed" needs a footnote, and it catches people out. There are two separate events:

  • The gambling win itself — not taxed, same as any recreational win.
  • The crypto as an asset — IRD treats cryptocurrency as taxable property. If the coins rise in value between when you acquired them and when you sell back to NZD, that gain can be taxable income, separately from the gambling.

A worked example: you buy $500 of Bitcoin, gamble with it and win, then later the Bitcoin you hold is worth more when you convert back to NZD. The pokies win is tax-free — but the increase in the Bitcoin's value while you held it may be taxable. The gambling and the crypto price movement are two different things, and only the second can create a tax liability. Our crypto casinos guide covers this in more depth.

Keep it simple: if you'd rather not deal with the property angle at all, stablecoins (USDT/USDC) hold a steady value, so there's little or no price gain to worry about while you play. Our crypto casinos guide has the full detail.

Gifting winnings, benefits and reporting

Gifting your winnings

New Zealand abolished gift duty in 2011, so gifting some of a tax-free win to family generally doesn't create a gift tax. Be aware that large gifts can still interact with other rules — for example, if you receive a benefit or a residential-care subsidy, gifting can affect asset assessments. That's a means-testing question, not a gambling-tax one, but it's worth advice before you give away a big win.

Do you have to declare winnings?

For a recreational player, no. Winnings aren't assessable income, so there's nothing to put in a tax return and nothing is withheld when you cash out. You'd only account for tax on the separate angles above — a genuine gambling business, crypto price gains, or interest earned on the money once you bank it.

Benefits and Work and Income

If you receive a benefit, a one-off gambling or lottery win can still need to be reported to Work and Income because it may affect your entitlement — again, that's a benefit-rules matter, not income tax. The win stays tax-free; the reporting is about means-tested support.

Anti-money-laundering checks

Cashing out a large win can trigger identity and source-of-funds checks under anti-money-laundering rules. That's a compliance process at the casino or bank, not a tax — it doesn't make your winnings taxable, but keeping simple records of a big win helps you clear the checks smoothly.

How New Zealand compares to other countries

New Zealand's tax-free treatment of recreational gambling isn't unusual — the UK, Australia, Canada and Ireland broadly don't tax recreational players either, on the same logic that winnings are windfalls rather than income. The United States is the notable outlier, taxing gambling winnings as income with withholding at source. For Kiwis, the practical takeaway is simple: a win here is yours to keep, and the only things that can create a tax bill are running a gambling business, the crypto-property rule, or the income your winnings later earn.

What records should you keep?

You don't need to track ordinary recreational winnings for tax — there's nothing to declare. But keeping light records is smart in two cases:

  • If you use crypto — note what you paid for the coins (date, amount, NZD value) and what you converted back, so you can work out any taxable gain.
  • If your play could look like a business — records of stakes, wins and losses help you or an accountant assess whether the professional-gambler test could apply.

For everyone else, a win is a win. Enjoy it — and set some aside for the fun of the next session, not the taxman.
