NZ Online Casino Law 2026 · DIA Licensing & Legal Play Explained

New Zealand is opening a licensed online-casino market for the first time. Here's what the Gambling Act 2003 actually says, how the new Department of Internal Affairs licensing regime works, the key dates, and what changes for you as a player.

Quick answer: Playing at an online casino has always been legal for the player in New Zealand under the Gambling Act 2003 — what was banned was a NZ-based company running one. Now the Online Casino Gambling Act is creating a licensed market: the Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) will make available up to around 15 operator licences (max three per operator), with an auction stage in 2026, applications due 1 December 2026, and licensed sites following into 2027. Until then, offshore casinos stay legal to play, and they'll continue alongside the new regulated options.

Where the law starts: the Gambling Act 2003

The Gambling Act 2003 is the foundation for everything below, administered by the Department of Internal Affairs. For online casinos, three points matter most:

  • Playing offshore is legal for the individual. It has never been an offence for a New Zealander to gamble at an overseas online casino. Millions of Kiwis have done so legally for years.
  • NZ-based online casinos were prohibited. Under the Act, remote interactive gambling is prohibited unless exempt, so a New Zealand company couldn't run an online casino for locals — the whole market operated offshore.
  • Advertising offshore gambling to New Zealanders was restricted, which is why you rarely saw casino ads on local TV or radio.

That created an odd situation: playing was fine, but there was no NZ regulator overseeing the sites Kiwis actually used, no local licensing, and no NZ tax being collected on a grey market estimated at hundreds of millions of dollars a year. The new law is designed to fix that.

What changes: the Online Casino Gambling Act

The Online Casino Gambling Act creates, for the first time, a licensed online-casino market in New Zealand, regulated by the DIA. The headline mechanics:

  • Up to around 15 operator licences will be made available.
  • No single operator may hold more than three licences (one per platform brand).
  • Licences are allocated through a competitive process including an auction stage in 2026, so operators bid to win one of the limited slots.
  • Applications are due 1 December 2026.
  • Licensed sites launch after the auction and DIA approval, into 2027, once each operator meets its conditions. Licence terms run up to three years, renewable.
  • Expect binding requirements around player protection, harm minimisation, age verification (18+) and advertising limits as conditions of holding a licence.
Why a capped, auctioned market? Limiting the number of licences lets the DIA vet operators closely and keep the market accountable, while the auction raises revenue and signals that only well-resourced, compliant operators get in. It's a deliberately controlled opening — not a free-for-all.

Tax, duties and community funding

The regime is built to keep more value in New Zealand. The offshore gambling duty on operators is being lifted (from 12% toward 16% of gross gambling revenue), and a slice of revenue — around 4% of GGR — is earmarked for community grants, with early projections in the range of NZ$10–20 million a year. Licensed operators pay tax like any other New Zealand business. The point is to replace some of the funding that leaked offshore while the grey market went untaxed.

Advertising, enforcement and penalties

The DIA gets real teeth. Under the new regime it can issue take-down notices, formal warnings and enforceable undertakings, with financial penalties of up to NZ$5 million for serious or repeated breaches. Advertising is tightly staged: an advertising ban on unlicensed offshore platforms is set to commence around 1 May 2026, ahead of the licensing process, and licensed operators will only be able to advertise later, gradually and with restrictions. Offshore operators that don't apply by the deadline face obligations to cease serving New Zealanders and the same penalty exposure.

What it means for you as a player

Nothing you're doing today becomes illegal. In practical terms:

  • Offshore play stays legal meanwhile. Until licensed NZ sites are live, playing at an offshore online casino remains legal for you under the Gambling Act 2003.
  • Expect NZ-regulated options to appear. As the DIA licenses operators, you'll start to see sites that answer to a New Zealand regulator — with clearer complaint paths and enforced harm-minimisation rules.
  • Both will run side by side, at least for a while. Offshore sites won't vanish overnight; the two markets will coexist as licensing rolls out.
  • Watch for NZ-specific protections. Licensed sites are likely to bake in deposit limits, self-exclusion and stronger ID checks as licence conditions — see our responsible gambling page for the tools to use either way.

Casinos vs sports betting — opposite directions

It's easy to mix these up, but NZ's two recent gambling changes head opposite ways. Online casinos are being opened up. Sports and race betting was locked down in 2025 — offshore bookmakers were banned from taking NZ bets, and the TAB keeps its monopoly on betting.

Class 4 pokies and the wider gambling picture

The online casino law sits alongside New Zealand's existing gambling classes, and it's worth knowing the difference. Class 4 gambling is the non-casino gaming machines — the pokies in pubs and clubs — licensed under the Gambling Act 2003 and run to raise money for community grants. Land-based casinos (like SkyCity and Christchurch Casino) are licensed separately again. The new regime is specifically about online casino gaming, a category that until now had no NZ licensing at all. So "is online gambling legal in NZ?" depends on which product you mean — Class 4 pokies and land casinos have long been regulated locally, betting is a TAB monopoly, and online casinos are the piece finally being brought under NZ oversight.

Tax: recreational wins aren't taxed

Good news for players: recreational casino winnings are not taxed in New Zealand — no income tax and no GST on a normal win. The main exception is cryptocurrency, which the IRD treats as taxable property, so gains on the coin itself can be taxable even when the gambling win is not.
